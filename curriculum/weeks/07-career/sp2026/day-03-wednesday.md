# Day 3: Tap Your Network

Your profiles are tight. Your story is clear. Now it's time to do the thing that actually gets you hired: talk to people.

This is not "networking" in the cringe, LinkedIn-comment, "great post!" sense. This is about genuinely connecting with people who might be able to help you — and who you might be able to help. Your next opportunity is almost certainly going to come through another person. Not a job board. Not an application portal. A person.

Today you're going to identify every person in your world who might be relevant, figure out who to talk to first, craft messages that are actually worth sending, and start sending them. You're also going to set up a tracking system that you'll use for the rest of the week — and beyond.

## Part 1: Map Your Network

Before you can reach out to anyone, you need to see the full landscape of who you know. Most people massively underestimate the size of their network. You think you "don't know anyone" — but you do. You just haven't thought about it systematically.

### The Exercise: Stream-of-Consciousness List

Set a timer for 20 minutes. Open a new file in your repo (e.g., `network-map.md`). Write down every single person you can think of who might have even the slightest relevance to your career search. Don't filter. Don't judge. Just list names.

Go through these categories one by one to jog your memory:

- **Former colleagues and managers** — Anyone you've worked with professionally. Even if it was years ago. Even if you weren't close.
- **Classmates and cohort-mates** — People from this program, from school, from any other educational experience.
- **Friends who work in tech** — Or adjacent to tech. Or who know people in tech.
- **Friends and family who work anywhere interesting** — Your uncle who works at a bank. Your friend's partner who's at a startup. Cast wide.
- **People you've interacted with online** — Twitter mutuals, Discord communities, open-source contributors you've talked to, people whose blog posts you've commented on.
- **People you admire but haven't talked to** — Authors, speakers, founders, engineers whose work you follow. These are cold outreach candidates, not warm leads — but list them anyway. Use the [Apollo.io Chrome extension](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/apolloio-free-b2b-phone-n/alhgpfoeiimagjlnfekdhkjlkiomcapa?hl=en-US) to find corporate contact info for people you can't reach through LinkedIn or mutual connections.
- **Recruiters who've reached out to you** — Check your LinkedIn inbox. Check your email. Anyone who's ever pinged you about a role.
- **People from meetups, conferences, or events** — Even if you exchanged two sentences. If you can remember their name, write it down. And if you're not already plugged into the NYC tech events scene, subscribe to these newsletters and start showing up:
  - [Gary's Guide](https://www.garysguide.com/events?region=nyc) — NYC tech events aggregator
  - [nextNYC](https://www.next.nyc/) — curated NYC tech events
  - [This Week in the NYC Innovation Community](https://www.nycweeklynewsletter.com/) — weekly newsletter
  - [NYC Tech Alliance](https://www.nytech.org/events) — events from the NYC tech nonprofit
- **Former clients, customers, or users** — Anyone who's experienced your work from the other side.
- **Instructors, mentors, advisors** — Anyone who's helped you learn.

For each person, write their name and one line about why they're on the list — what the connection is, what they do, why they might be relevant. Don't overthink it. This is a braindump.

### Tag Each Person: Beneficiary, Helper, or Superconnector

As you build your list, tag each person with at least one of these labels (from [Alterman's Outreach Workbook](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1gif1am4T0CRESkXKiK6RjaciRciwTfjRoM-7ctnp34A/edit?tab=t.0)):

- **Beneficiary** — Someone who would genuinely benefit from connecting with you. Maybe they need what you can build. Maybe they're hiring for a role you'd be great at. Maybe you can offer them something valuable. This reframe matters: you're not just asking for favors, you're identifying people who _get something_ from this interaction too.
- **Helper** — Someone who'd be excited to help you, even if they don't directly benefit. Don't underestimate this category. A lot of people — way more than you think — genuinely enjoy helping others, even people they're not close to. Cast wide here: include people who aren't in tech, people who aren't connected to your target roles, anyone who generally wants to help you with _anything_. You might be surprised by what resources they have to throw your way. And if they're not interested, they'll just not respond. That's fine. So go ask.
- **Superconnector** — Someone who takes pride in connecting people. They know everyone. They love making introductions. Even if you only met them once at a party or event, superconnectors often don't mind getting asks from near-strangers — connecting people is what they _do_. Your ask gives them value to offer someone in their network. Make a specific sub-list of superconnectors you know — you'll message them differently (more on this in Part 3).

## Part 2: Prioritize — Warmth and Excitement

You now have a big list. You can't talk to everyone at once. You need to figure out who to reach out to first.

### The Two Dimensions

For each person on your list, think about two things:

1. **Warmth** — How strong is your existing relationship? Would this person recognize your name? Would they be happy to hear from you? Do they have any reason to want to help you?
   - **Hot:** You've worked together, you're friends, they've helped you before, they'd take your call today.
   - **Warm:** You've met, you have some shared context, they'd probably remember you. Might need a reminder of who you are.
   - **Cool:** You have a loose connection — a mutual friend, a shared community, a brief interaction once. You'd need an introduction or a good reason to reach out.
   - **Cold:** No existing relationship. You admire their work or want to work at their company, but they don't know you exist.

2. **Excitement** — How genuinely interested are you in talking to this person? Not "would this be strategically useful" — do you actually want to have this conversation?
   - If you're excited to talk to someone, the conversation will be better. You'll be more engaged, more curious, more authentic. That energy is contagious. People want to help people who are genuinely interested in them.

### The Prioritization

Start with the people who are both **warm and exciting**. These are your first outreach targets. The conversations will be easy to start and energizing to have.

Then move to **warm but less exciting** — these are still easy conversations to start, and you might be surprised. Sometimes the most useful conversations come from people you didn't expect.

Then **cool but exciting** — these require more craft in your outreach, but the excitement will carry you through.

Save **cold outreach** for last, and be strategic about it. Cold outreach has a low hit rate, so you want to be very targeted.

Mark each person on your list with a priority: **1** (reach out today), **2** (reach out this week), **3** (reach out eventually). Aim for at least 15-20 people marked as priority 1.

## Part 3: Craft Genuine Asks

This is the hard part. Writing a message to someone asking for help — or even just to reconnect — feels vulnerable. Most people either avoid it entirely or default to something generic and forgettable. You're going to do neither.

### The Three-Draft Process

For each person you're reaching out to, write three versions of the message:

**Draft 1: The Vulnerable Truth**

Write the most honest version of what you want to say. Don't worry about how it sounds. Don't optimize for anything. Just write what's actually true.

_"Hey, I'm going through a career transition and I'm honestly kind of scared. I saw that you're working at [company] and I thought maybe you could help me figure out if I'd be a good fit there. I don't really know what I'm doing with this whole networking thing but you seemed like someone who'd be willing to talk."_

You're probably not going to send this version. That's fine. The point is to get to what's real. Most bad outreach messages are bad because the person writing them is performing instead of communicating. This draft gets the performance out of the way.

**Draft 2: The Empathy Flip**

Now put yourself in the recipient's shoes. They're busy. They get messages like this sometimes. What would make them _want_ to respond?

Think about:

- What do they care about? What are they working on? What are they proud of?
- What would genuinely delight them to hear from you?
- What specific thing could you ask that they'd find interesting to answer — not burdensome, but actually engaging?
- How can you make it easy for them to say yes?

The key insight: the best outreach messages make the recipient feel good, not obligated. "I've been following your work on X and I had a specific question about Y" is infinitely better than "Can I pick your brain?"

**Draft 3: The Actual Message**

Combine the honesty of Draft 1 with the empathy of Draft 2. This is the one you send.

Guidelines:

- **Keep it short.** 3-5 sentences for most messages. Respect their time.
- **Be specific.** About why you're reaching out to _them_, not just anyone. About what you're asking for. About what you admire or find interesting about their work.
- **Make the ask clear and small.** "Would you be open to a 15-minute call this week?" is better than "I'd love to connect sometime." A specific ask gets a specific answer.
- **Include a scheduling link.** Set up a [Cal.com](https://cal.com) or [Calendly](https://calendly.com) link (or use Google Calendar's appointment scheduling) so people can book time with you directly. When someone says "sure, let's chat," you want to send them a link — not start a five-message back-and-forth about availability. Set this up before you start sending messages.
- **Don't apologize for reaching out.** No "sorry to bother you" or "I know you're busy." You're offering them a chance to help someone — most people like doing that.
- **Don't write a cover letter.** This is a human message, not a formal communication. Write like you talk.

### Build a Reusable Blurb

The three-draft process is for your most important, personalized messages. But you also need a way to scale. You can't write three drafts for 50 people.

This is where the **blurb** comes in (from [Alterman's Outreach Workbook](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1gif1am4T0CRESkXKiK6RjaciRciwTfjRoM-7ctnp34A/edit?tab=t.0)). A blurb is a short, reusable template message about who you are and what you're looking for — one you can quickly customize for different audiences.

**How to write a good blurb:**

1. **Keep it SHORT.** Making it exhaustively long is a rookie move. People can click through or ask questions if they want to know more.
2. **One clear call-to-action.** "Would you be open to a 15-minute call?" or "Could you forward this to anyone who might be hiring?" — not both.
3. **Add urgency.** Not fake urgency, but real context: "I'm actively interviewing right now" or "I'm wrapping up a program next week and starting my search."
4. **Social proof if you have it.** "I just finished an AI accelerator program" or "I've been building with [technology] for X months" — anything that makes the reader take you more seriously.

Write your blurb as if you're texting a specific friend. That keeps it from sounding like a mass email. Then create **variants** for different audiences:

- One for former colleagues (shared context, casual tone)
- One for people in your target industry (more about what you can do)
- One for superconnectors (see below)
- One for posting in communities (see Part 4)

### Superconnector Messages: The Forwardable Blurb

Superconnectors need a different kind of message. You're not just asking them to talk to you — you're asking them to connect you with people in _their_ network. That means your message needs to be **forwardable**: something they can copy-paste or share with minimal effort.

The structure:

1. **Short personal note to the superconnector** — remind them who you are, what you're looking for, and why you're reaching out to them specifically.
2. **A blurb they can forward** — a self-contained paragraph about you that makes sense to someone who's never met you. Include what you do, what you're looking for, and how to reach you.

Example structure:

> _Hey [name], I'm currently looking for [type of role] and I know you know everyone. Would you be open to forwarding the message below to anyone in your network who might be relevant?_
>
> _[Forwardable blurb: "Hi, I'm [name]. I'm a [background] looking for [role type]. I recently [impressive thing]. I'm especially interested in [specific area]. Happy to chat — here's my calendar link: [link]. Resume attached / LinkedIn: [link]."]_

This makes it dead easy for the superconnector to help you. They don't have to write anything — just forward.

## Part 4: Send, Go Wide, and Track

### The Career Action Log

Create a new file in your repo: `career-action-log.md` (or a spreadsheet — whatever you'll actually maintain). This is your running record of every career-related action you take from now through the end of the week. You'll present this on Saturday.

For each action, log:

| Date       | Action Type | Who                                | What You Did                                                  | What Happened | Notes                              |
| ---------- | ----------- | ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| 2026-03-25 | Outreach    | Jane Smith (former colleague at X) | Sent LinkedIn message asking for 15-min call about roles at Y | Pending       | She's now engineering manager at Y |

Action types include: **Outreach sent**, **Conversation had**, **Application submitted**, **Connection made**, **Follow-up sent**, **Coffee/call scheduled**, **Referral received**, **Job lead identified**.

Include the actual text of messages you sent. This is part of the artifact — on Saturday, you'll review each other's outreach and give feedback on the messages themselves.

### The 100 Conversations Challenge

Your goal is to have 100 conversations. You will not hit 100 today — or even this week. This is a long-running quest. But the tracker starts now, and every conversation counts.

Why 100? It's a number big enough to change you. By conversation 30, you'll have learned things about the market you couldn't have learned any other way. By conversation 60, you'll have opportunities you didn't know existed. By 100, you'll have a completely different relationship with the process of finding work. The point isn't the number — it's the transformation that happens when you commit to enough volume that you stop overthinking each individual interaction.

### 10 Daily Career Actions

Starting today and for the rest of the week, aim for at least 10 career-related actions per day. A "career action" is anything that moves the needle:

- Sending an outreach message
- Having a conversation (call, coffee, DM exchange)
- Submitting an application
- Following up on a previous outreach
- Making a new connection
- Scheduling a call
- Researching a company you want to target
- Improving a specific artifact based on feedback from a conversation

Log every single one. The log is the proof. On Saturday, when you present your career report, the log is what shows whether you actually did the work or just thought about doing it.

### Now: Send Messages

You have your prioritized list. You have your drafted messages. Start sending.

Set a goal for today: **send at least 10 messages.** They don't all have to be perfectly crafted three-draft masterpieces. The first few should be — use the three-draft process for the people who matter most. But after that, it's okay to move faster. A good message sent is infinitely better than a perfect message sitting in your drafts.

As you send, log every one in your career action log.

### Go Beyond 1:1: Audience and Channel Outreach

Everything so far has been one-to-one messaging. But there's a whole other mode of outreach: **one-to-many**. You can reach dozens or hundreds of relevant people by posting in the right places.

**Step 1: Brainstorm channel types.** What kinds of groups or communities would contain people relevant to your search? Think abstractly first: "engineer job-seeker Slack groups," "NYC tech Discord servers," "startup-focused LinkedIn groups," "alumni networks," etc.

**Step 2: Identify specific channels.** Turn abstract categories into concrete targets — specific Discord servers, Slack workspaces, LinkedIn groups, subreddits, Facebook groups, WhatsApp group chats, Meetup groups, newsletters with job boards. How to find these:

- Start with the community suggestions Claude gave you in Part 1 (the "Online communities" item from your network-expansion prompt). Now look at those through a different lens: not just "where can I find people?" but "where can I _post_ about my search?"
- Ask friends for suggestions — especially your superconnectors
- Google around

**Step 3: Post in them.** Join the channels. Adapt your blurb for each community's vibe and norms. A message for a casual Discord server reads differently than a post in a LinkedIn group. Some communities have dedicated #jobs or #introductions channels — use them. For channels where you're not a member, reach out to the organizer and ask if they'd share your message. Cold outreach to group organizers is totally normal — they're used to it.

**Don't forget your own channels.** You already have audiences — use them. Post about your job search on:

- **LinkedIn** — this is the obvious one. A genuine, non-cringe post about what you're looking for gets shared more than you'd expect.
- **Twitter/X** — especially if you have any tech following.
- **Instagram stories** — your non-tech friends know people too.
- **Group chats you're already in** — friends, alumni, hobby groups. A quick "hey, I'm looking for X, know anyone?" in a group chat you're already in is zero-cost, high-upside.

Posts that tell a story get more engagement than posts that just ask for help. "I just finished an intensive AI program and I'm looking for my next role — here's what I built" works better than "Looking for a software engineering role, DM me."

### Follow Up — The Most Underrated Move

**This applies to everything above.** You sent a message and didn't get a response? Follow up. This is not optional — it's the difference between people who get results from outreach and people who don't.

Here's the reality: the person you messaged is probably busy, gets bombarded by notifications, and may have seen your message and genuinely intended to respond but forgot. A follow-up is not annoying — it's a signal that you actually care.

**When to follow up:**

- **Warm contacts:** 3-5 days after your initial message if no response.
- **Cold contacts / busy people (CEOs, hiring managers):** 2-3 days.
- **Community posts:** Repost or bump after a week if the community allows it.

**How to follow up:**

- Keep it light. "Hey, just bumping this in case it got buried!" works fine.
- If you have something new to add, even better: "Following up — I also just shipped [thing], figured it might be relevant."
- Don't write a whole new message. One or two sentences is enough.

**How many times?** At least twice. If someone doesn't respond after two follow-ups, move on. But most people give up after _zero_ follow-ups — don't be most people.

Track your follow-ups in your career action log. "Follow-up sent" is a valid career action, and seeing which follow-ups convert to conversations is valuable data.

## Part 5: Keep the Momentum — Mandatory Celebration

This work is hard. Not technically hard — emotionally hard. Putting yourself out there, asking for help, risking rejection or silence. Most people avoid it because it's uncomfortable. The fact that you're doing it at all puts you ahead of most job seekers.

So celebrate it. Seriously.

Every time you send a message, log it and take a moment to acknowledge that you did something most people won't do. Every time you get a response — even a "sorry, can't help" — that's contact with reality. Every conversation you have is data you didn't have before.

If you hit a wall and don't want to send any more messages, that's fine. Log it honestly. Write down what stopped you. Then figure out if you can send one more. Just one. The habit of pushing through the discomfort is more valuable than any individual message.

If you're feeling energized and the messages are flowing, ride that wave. Don't cap yourself at 10 if you've got momentum for 20.

At the end of the day, review your log. Count your actions. Note what worked — which messages felt easy to write, which conversations went well, which approaches got responses. Note what didn't work — where you got stuck, what felt forced, what you're avoiding. This reflection is fuel for tomorrow.
