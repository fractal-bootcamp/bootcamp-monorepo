# Day 4: Spearfishing (Day 1)

Your profiles are tight. Your story is clear. You've been talking to people and logging every action. Now it's time to aim.

Broad networking creates opportunities through volume and serendipity. Spearfishing is the opposite: you pick one company, study what they care about, and build something that demonstrates exactly why they should hire you. It's not a generic portfolio piece. It's a love letter to a specific opportunity — an audition that says, "here is what it would actually feel like if I worked on your product for a day."

This works because of how hiring actually functions. Most applicants barely read the job description. Almost no one does meaningful work tailored to a company's situation. When someone sends a working prototype that feels like it belongs inside their product, it's a shock — and it flips the decision from "will this random resume waste my time?" to "do I want more of the kind of initiative this person just showed me?"

Pre-AI, this was hard to recommend — spending a week or more on each attempt with no guarantee anyone would even open your email. Post-AI, the equation is different. You already know how to build with AI. You can turn a well-chosen idea into a convincing demo in hours, not weeks. Today you'll pick your target, brainstorm and spec a demo, and start building. Tomorrow you'll finish, record a video walkthrough, and send it.

One more thing: even if a spearfish doesn't land you a job, the process itself is valuable. You're going to spend a day immersing yourself in a company's product, understanding their users, and learning a new problem space. That's interesting work. You'll come out of it knowing more about an industry, a technology, or a business than you did going in — and that knowledge compounds. The best spearfishes come from genuine curiosity, not obligation.

## Part 1: Pick Your Target

Spearfishing only works when the person reading your email can realistically say "yes" or at least "let's talk." That means you need to be strategic about who you target.

**Where spearfishing works best:**

- **Founders at early-stage companies** — they can create roles, design internship deals, fast-track someone based purely on a demo. Maximum flexibility.
- **Hiring managers / tech leads at small or mid-size startups** — they can usually decide *who* to hire, even if headcount timing is a constraint. A strong spearfish can bypass the resume queue.
- **Companies that are actively hiring** — so your effort has the best chance of converting to something real.

**Where spearfishing is mostly wasted:**

- **Large companies with centralized HR** (Google, Meta, etc.) — managers almost never bypass the official pipeline, especially for junior roles. They might love your demo and still be physically unable to hire you.

### The Exercise: Build Your Prospect List

By the end of this step, you should have a list of **3-10 companies** that meet these conditions:

- You can imagine being at least somewhat excited to work on their product.
- You can imagine building *some kind* of demo for them.
- They are not so large and rigid that no one can act on your email.
- There is at least a weak signal that they hire engineers right now.

**Where to find candidates:**

- **People you've already talked to.** Look at your career action log from yesterday. Did any conversations spark excitement about a company? Did anyone mention they're hiring?
- **Products you already use.** Tools, apps, APIs you touch regularly and have opinions about. Being a real user gives you immediate ideas for realistic demos.
- **Spaces and problems you care about.** Look back at your journal and marketing questions — what domains genuinely excite you? Look for startups in those spaces.
- **Your network map.** Who on your list works somewhere interesting? A spearfish plus a warm introduction from someone at the company is an extremely powerful combination.
- **Curated job boards.** YC's Work at a Startup, "Made in New York," niche startup boards. If a company is hiring for a role that matches your lane, a strong spearfish can plausibly bypass their resume queue.

### Quick Filter

For each company on your rough list, ask yourself:

- Would I be willing to spend a full day building a demo for this company?
- Can I think of at least one plausible angle for a demo?
- Is this company small and flexible enough that a real person could act on my email?
- Can I identify a specific person to send it to — a founder, engineering manager, or someone publicly identified with the company? Use the [Apollo.io Chrome extension](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/apolloio-free-b2b-phone-n/alhgpfoeiimagjlnfekdhkjlkiomcapa?hl=en-US) to find corporate contact info for people you can't reach through LinkedIn or mutual connections.

If the honest answer to any of these is "no," remove that company from your list. You are aiming for a **short, honest list**, not a comprehensive one.

## Part 2: Brainstorm Demo Ideas

You've picked your target. Now you need to figure out what to build.

A good spearfish demo is not a generic portfolio piece that happens to use their API. It's an audition. It should:

- **Use their product or APIs, or live directly in their problem space.** Show that you understand what they're building.
- **Show that you understand what they're trying to do for their users.** You're not just calling an API at random — you've thought about their users and their goals.
- **Be something they would plausibly find interesting, useful, or delightful.** The hiring manager should look at it and think, "That's actually clever."

### The Exercise: Sketch Ideas

For your target company, sketch **2-3 demo ideas**. Each one should be:

- Something you could build in about a day, using technologies you already know.
- Clearly understandable in 30-60 seconds.
- Obviously connected to their product, users, or problem space.

You don't need a full spec yet. You need rough shapes:

- "A small tool on top of their API that does X for Y kind of user."
- "A simplified and improved version of their current onboarding flow."
- "A tiny companion app that shows a fresh, interesting way to use their product."
- "An AI-powered feature that solves a problem their users clearly have."

**Things to look at for inspiration:**

- Their product itself — use it. Where are the rough edges? What's missing? What could be better?
- Their changelog or blog — what have they been working on? What direction are they heading?
- Their open-source repos (if any) — what issues are open? What's the community asking for?
- Their users' complaints — check Twitter, Reddit, product review sites. What do people wish was different?
- AI opportunities — a lot of companies are behind the curve on AI. Can you imagine ways AI could improve their product or solve one of their problems?

### A Real Example

Andrei, a student in Su2025, wanted to work at ElevenLabs. After attending one of their events, he noticed that their text-to-speech, when sped up, could sound better. He built a small demo — [smart-speed.vercel.app](https://smart-speed.vercel.app/) — to help listen to written articles faster using their API. The email he sent with this demo got him to the final round of interviews at ElevenLabs. The demo was small, focused, clearly connected to their product, and showed he'd actually used and thought about their technology.

### Pick Your Strongest Idea

From your 2-3 sketches, pick the one that:

- Feels most compelling and concrete to you.
- Has the clearest "aha" moment — one main thing the demo does that is obvious and memorable.
- You're confident you can build in a day.

That's your spearfish.

## Part 3: Spec the Demo

Before you start coding, get precise about what someone will see and understand in the first 30-60 seconds of looking at your demo.

The 60-second constraint matters for two reasons:

- **From their side:** they don't owe you more than a minute of attention.
- **From your side:** spearfishing is a probabilistic game. Small, sharp bets beat one gigantic mega-project.

### Write a Short Spec

Your spec should contain:

- **Who the demo is for** — what type of user.
- **What they can do with it** — the core interaction.
- **How it relates to the company's existing product** — the connection should be obvious.
- **The "aha" moment** — a clear statement like: "A new user lands on this page, clicks once or twice, and sees X happen, which is interesting because Y."

### Common Traps

- **Scoping to "half their product"** instead of one narrow flow. You're building a demo, not a competitor.
- **Scoping so small it feels trivial** — a slight CSS tweak is not a spearfish.
- **Designing something that requires a long tutorial** — the demo must be self-explanatory. No README required.

>
> Then write me a concrete spec I can start building from — screens, interactions, data flow. Keep it tight.

## Part 4: Build

Now you actually build the thing. The goal is not to engineer something impressive; the goal is to ship something clear.

### How to Think About Building

- **Use boring, familiar tech.** This is not the moment to learn a new stack. Use what you know so you can move fast.
- **The demo must work reliably.** Someone should be able to click into it and see the main interaction without debugging. Broken demos are worse than no demo.
- **Clarity over cleverness.** A simple demo that's instantly understandable beats an impressive one that requires explanation.
- **Ship, don't polish.** It's fine if the code is somewhat messy under the hood, as long as the demo behaves clearly and doesn't break. You're shipping a demo, not a production system.

### The Workflow

The most reliable approach:

1. Take your spec from Part 3 and break it into small, concrete implementation steps.
2. Work through each step, using AI to help you move fast.
3. Deploy early. Get it live on Vercel (or wherever you deploy) as soon as you have something working, even if it's rough. Then iterate on the live version.
4. Test the 30-second experience. Open your deployed demo in an incognito window and pretend you've never seen it. Can you understand what it does in 30 seconds? If not, simplify.

### Common Traps

- **Getting lost in infrastructure** (auth, databases, deployment) when you could fake or stub those parts. A demo doesn't need a real backend if a mock will do.
- **Polishing minor UI details** while the core interaction is still unclear. Get the "aha" moment working first, then polish.
- **Treating this as a capstone project.** This is a small, sharp artifact. One concentrated day of building. If you're thinking about it in terms of weeks, you've overscoped.

### What "Done for Today" Looks Like

By the end of today, you should have:

- [ ] A deployed, working demo (even if rough around the edges)
- [ ] The core "aha" moment functioning — someone can click through and see the main thing
- [ ] A clear sense of what still needs polish tomorrow before you record your video walkthrough

If you're ahead of schedule, start thinking about the email you'll send (that's tomorrow's work, but it doesn't hurt to draft early). If you're behind, don't panic — cut scope. A small, working demo beats an ambitious, broken one every time.

## Keep Networking

Spearfishing and networking run in parallel. Don't stop your daily career actions just because you're building.

**Today's targets:**

- [ ] At least 10 career actions logged (messages sent, conversations had, follow-ups, etc.)
- [ ] Career action log updated
- [ ] Follow up on any unanswered messages from yesterday — this is the most underrated move in outreach. Most people give up after zero follow-ups. Don't be most people.

If you're deep in a build and can't context-switch to networking, batch your outreach: take 30 minutes in the morning and 30 minutes in the evening to send messages and log actions. The build is important, but the network is what creates the opportunities the build is designed to target.
